# Judge questions and answers

1. **What would a mission team do with this?** Start with the regional shortlist, open the imagery and evidence for each location, and pick where to investigate mobility, slope or landing terrain further. A field decision would also need access, geology and local safety checks.
2. **What exactly do you measure?** The shape of the terrain inside cells of about 2 km: slopes over 240 m and 570 m, roughness over 200 m to 1.6 km, and the share and depth of closed depressions. Features smaller than about 100 m, including boulders, small craters and the slopes a rover or astronaut meets, are not measured. Weather, regolith, radiation and illumination matter to field suitability and need separate evidence.
3. **How is NASA data central?** NASA LOLA provides the lunar elevation reference and a published check of lunar slopes. Independent ICESat-2 tracks check Earth grid slopes. NASA Earth imagery (Worldview, HLS), meteorological data and LRO instrument products give context for interpreting matches.
4. **Does the slope test validate the whole ranking?** The NASA slope-map test measures agreement for a dedicated plane-fit estimator. The ranking uses two-point slopes along with roughness and depressions. Each check has its own scope.
5. **Why compare laser profiles and grids?** Laser tracks sample along directions and across footprints that differ from the grid. ICESat-2 gives an independent Earth comparison, with laser-to-grid slope ratios of 0.43–1.16 at 240 m for the selected sites.
6. **What happens when you change the Earth elevation data?** Copernicus and NASADEM produced similar slopes while depression and candidate maps differed. On their common coverage, Spearman was 0.637 and 21 of the top 50 tiles were shared. Copernicus remains the primary input under the rule written before the comparison.
7. **What did the known analog test tell you?** At the site scale, over 22 known lunar analogs (tagged lunar or both), the median enrichment was 1.02× the scan-wide bare-cell rate, versus 0.39× for matched random circles; 11 of the 22 lie above the rate, which equals the 95th percentile of random runs, and 2.8% of random runs reach our median. That is borderline. At the one-degree tile scale, the recovery AUC was 0.531 with a 90% interval of 0.396–0.668, consistent with chance.
   **But were those analogs chosen for their terrain?** Mostly not. By the opened source pages, 4 of the 44 lunar known analogs were chosen for their terrain (Desert RATS and Apollo training sites near Flagstaff, and Devon Island); 20 for impact geology or rock type, 8 for climate, 3 for other reasons, 9 unknown. The terrain-chosen ones fall in only 2 tiles we can test, too few to say anything (the Flagstaff tile sits at the 22nd percentile). Those chosen for their material give AUC 0.460, also chance. So we do not claim the method finds analog terrain; we claim the listed sites have terrain statistics inside the lunar range. [Validation](../VALIDATION.md)
8. **How stable is the ranking?** Stable to the lunar sample: resampling the 1,575 lunar reference cells 200 times moves the top 10 by at most 3 places and a typical run keeps 28 of the top 30. Sensitive to the feature set: leaving roughness out keeps only 8 of the top 30, slopes 13, depressions 21. The ranking is a statement about the features together.
9. **Why do sand seas appear?** Dune hollows can register as depressions and their slopes can pass the similarity test. A terrain rule puts dune-like relief last. The temperature and moisture checks do not confirm that the flag marks dry sand, so it stays a terrain rule. [Surface-data validation](../sources/j26.md)
10. **Is the top of your ranking bare rock?** Not the default one. Our default land-cover mask counts ESA WorldCover "moss and lichen" as bare ground, so the Arctic plateaus at the top, such as the Putorana Plateau, are mostly tundra. With "bare / sparse vegetation" only, only 3 of our top 30 sites have a site of the strict top 30 within 3 km, and the strict top 30 is led by the Atacama and Puna, the western Tibetan Plateau and Ladakh, the coastal desert of southern Peru, the Hazen Plateau on northern Ellesmere Island and Mauna Loa. We show both rankings and treat the strict one as the conservative reading. [Validation](../VALIDATION.md)
11. **Are any of the places discoveries?** No. In the strict top 30, 9 sites lie in known analog regions, 12 in regions that an opened page names as a Mars analog (none as a Moon analog), and 9 were "not found in the analog catalogues and searches listed here" (six on the Chang Tang in Ngari, Tibet, Aksai Chin, and Acarí and Marcona in Peru). In the default top 30 the counts are 2, 10 and 18; southern Peary Land, our rank 1, is named as a Mars analogue in a 2025 thesis. The search was one to four English web queries per site row plus a second pass of analog catalogues, OpenAlex and Russian and Chinese searches for the leading regions, so "not found" is still a negative of limited strength, and these verdicts are an SI model's that a person has not yet confirmed. [Literature check](../NOVELTY.md)
12. **What about Mars?** The current reference covers the lunar south-pole regions. Applying this method to Mars would require choosing suitable Mars elevation data, recalibrating feature scales and validating the result against Mars-specific terrain and known Earth analogs. The literature hits of the strict top 30 are Mars-analog statements, which is a hint, not a test.
