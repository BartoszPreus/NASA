# A roughness spectrum and topology reveal terrestrial analogs of the Moon

Team Zodiak should compare Earth with the lunar south pole not by a single slope, but by a **feature vector computed with identical code on both bodies**: a roughness spectrum at baselines of 200/400/800/1600 m (the method of Barker et al. 2023, product PGDA 90), the Wasserstein distance between slope distributions, the share of closed depressions and drainage integration, and anisotropy, all after applying a bare-ground mask. This set answers the pilot result directly (slope alone is not selective, because forested mountains match too): amplitude features will always match some steep fluvial terrain, while the information that separates impact terrain from fluvial terrain is topological and directional. The most important methodological decision is to switch the lunar reference from the PGDA 70 maps (along-profile statistics, 57/225/560 m) to the gridded PGDA 90 products, because only these can be reproduced on an Earth DEM with the same estimator and verified against NASA data in 1-2 hours. Laser data enter in three roles: LOLA as the reference, airborne lidar DTMs of 1 m (USGS 3DEP, GUGiK NMT) as a quality check at a few sites, and ICESat-2 profiles through SlideRule as the only terrestrial route to the 57 m baseline; GEDI and MOLA are unsuitable. The Hurst exponent should be rejected as a feature: at the pole it has a median of 0.98 and an interquartile range of 0.94-0.99, so it separates nothing. Temperature, ice, gravity and vacuum go into the "where the analogy breaks down" field and are not scored. Three things need confirmation before the pitch: the official challenge text (the page could not be opened), the restructuring of the Artemis programme of February-March 2026 (secondary sources only), and the formulas for differential slope and curvature (given from the researcher's memory, without opening the source papers).

The reliability markers used throughout the report come from the notes and keep their distinctions: **OPENED** (page or PDF opened and read), **ABSTRACT ONLY** (only the abstract was read), **PARSED** (table read from a PDF extraction, column layout to be checked), **SNIPPET** (seen only in a search-result snippet, treat as unverified), **SECONDARY** (Wikipedia, press, third-party page), **UNVERIFIED / RECALL** (from the researcher's memory, source not opened), **LIVE CHECK** (anonymous HTTP request of 14 Nov 2026), **INFERENCE** (reasoning or arithmetic of the researchers, not a quote) and **TEAM** (the team's own finding on data). Numbers with a marker other than OPENED, PARSED or LIVE CHECK should not go into the pitch or the README as certain.

## The two lunar references measure different things and must not be mixed

The PGDA 70 maps the team has worked with so far are the **median absolute point-to-point slope along the LRO track** at baselines of 57, 225 and 560 m, computed in overlapping 0.25° windows and interpolated to 16 pixels per degree in a cylindrical projection; the Hurst maps in the same product come from a fit to the deviogram over 57 m - 2.7 km (OPENED, [PGDA 70](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/70)). This is a one-dimensional, one-directional statistic. A slope computed on GLO-30 with a 3x3 kernel is instead the 2D gradient magnitude, and for isotropic terrain the median of the directional component is smaller than the median of the gradient magnitude by a factor of about 0.6-0.7 (INFERENCE: geometric expectation 2/π, not a measurement from the literature). Comparing these two quantities on one plot underestimates or overestimates the "lunar-ness" of Earth depending on the direction of the error. In addition, the cylindrical projection at 16 px/° is very coarse at the pole, so PGDA 70 describes "lunar highlands in general", not specific Artemis regions (INFERENCE from the notes on lidars).

The second reference, PGDA 90, is gridded and local. Barker et al. describe the method so that it can be re-implemented: every 50 m they fit a plane to the 20 m/px LDEM sampled at 81 points within half the baseline, for baselines of **100, 200, 400, 800 and 1600 m**, and define roughness as 1.48 x MAD of the height residuals from this plane; the product also contains the RMSD (centre point versus ring), the Hurst exponent, a k-means class from the roughness spectrum, the mean standardised roughness and the spectral slope (OPENED, [Barker et al. 2023, NTRS preprint](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230014419/downloads/BarkernewviewSTIupdated.pdf); OPENED, [PGDA 90](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/90)). All files are COG GeoTIFFs available without login, and anonymous access to `LPSR_80S_20MPP_ADJ.TIF`, `LDEM_80S_80MPP_ADJ.TIF` and the 5 m DEM of product 78 was confirmed by an HTTP 200 request (LIVE CHECK 14 Nov 2026, [PGDA 90](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/90)). The conclusion is unambiguous: **the reference for the south pole should be PGDA 90, and PGDA 70 stays as an auxiliary feature, computed on Earth as a two-point 1D slope along columns**, not as a gradient.

The lunar grid itself also has accuracy limits. "20 m/px" is the pixel size, not the resolution: the share of 20 m pixels with at least one LOLA measurement ranges from about 15% to about 75%, the rest is interpolated, and the effective resolution is about 20 m for the Artemis zone (south of 84°S) and a median of 56 m in the outermost tile of the analysis (OPENED, [Barker et al. 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230014419/downloads/BarkernewviewSTIupdated.pdf)). In the 5 m DEMs of product 78 about 90% of the pixels are interpolated, the median RMS height error is about 0.30-0.50 m, and slopes about 1.5-2.5° (OPENED, [PGDA 78](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/78)). Therefore every lunar feature should be computed only where the `_EFFRES` raster does not exceed about a quarter of the baseline (threshold proposed in the notes, INFERENCE).

The noise floor decides which baselines are fair on Earth. The GLO-30 specification gives a relative vertical accuracy below 2 m (LE90, point-to-point, slopes up to 20%) and explicitly calls the product a surface model including vegetation and buildings (OPENED, [Copernicus DEM Product Handbook v5.0](https://dataspace.copernicus.eu/sites/default/files/media/files/2024-06/geo1988-copernicusdem-spe-002_producthandbook_i5.0.pdf)). First-order propagation (INFERENCE, slope σ ≈ √2·σz/b) gives the picture below, in which the team's measured floor of 0.3-0.4° at 57 m lies below the upper bound from the specification:

| Height source | 57 m | 225 m | 560 m | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| LOLA, single track, range precision 0.1 m | 0.14° | 0.036° | 0.014° | INFERENCE from OPENED values (Barker 2023) |
| ICESat-2, segments, assumed 0.1 m per segment | 0.14° | 0.036° | 0.014° | INFERENCE; precision over bare rock UNVERIFIED |

The 57 m baseline is less than two GLO-30 pixels, so a "57 m slope" from this model is in practice an interpolated 60-90 m slope, underestimated on rough terrain and inflated by noise on smooth terrain (INFERENCE). **GLO-30 is therefore unusable at 57 m, borderline at 225 m on very smooth terrain and correct from about 200 m upwards on bare ground.** The Hurst instability the team observed has the same origin and additional confirmation in NASA data: for the south-pole grid, H fitted to baselines of 100-800 m has a median of 0.98 and an interquartile range of 0.94-0.99 (OPENED, [Barker et al. 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230014419/downloads/BarkernewviewSTIupdated.pdf)), while the values of Rosenburg et al. (0.95 highlands, 0.76 maria) come from another method and are known only from the abstract (SNIPPET, [doi:10.1029/2010JE003716](https://doi.org/10.1029/2010JE003716)). A global multifractal study moreover finds that Earth, Mars, Mercury and the Moon have the same scaling behaviour below about 10 km (ABSTRACT ONLY, [arXiv:1805.11249](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.11249)), which argues against scaling exponents as discriminating features.

## Catalogue of parameters: from the like-for-like core to what Earth cannot reproduce

The order in the table combines three criteria: whether the parameter can be computed with the same estimator on both bodies, whether it is robust at 30 m, and how much it adds beyond slope alone. The "impact/fluvial discrimination strength" rating is the researchers' reasoning; the notes say explicitly that **no published work was found that tests which DEM metrics separate impact from fluvial terrain between Earth and the Moon** (INFERENCE). Group C are quantities that no place on Earth reproduces and that are shown without a score: 1/6 g gravity, vacuum, radiation, electrostatics and angularity of grains, extreme thermal cycles. Foucher et al. write explicitly that conditions such as pressure, gravity and radiation cannot be reproduced at natural sites, and add an "analog limitation" field to each analog type (OPENED, [Foucher et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pss.2021.105162)). Mars is an option only at kilometre baselines: MOLA shots are about 300 m apart with a spot of about 160 m (SNIPPET, [ODE MOLA](https://ode.rsl.wustl.edu/mars/pagehelp/Content/Missions_Instruments/MGS/MOLA/Intro.htm)), and published roughness maps start at 0.6 km (SNIPPET, [doi:10.1029/2000JE001259](https://doi.org/10.1029/2000JE001259)), so a common method for three bodies exists only from about 0.6-1 km (INFERENCE). GEDI is out: one regional example gives a ground-height RMSE of 5-7 m (SNIPPET, [Unitbv repository](https://repository.unitbv.ro/bitstreams/6ca31893-b496-4aa6-9b28-805233ff85ea/download)), which at a 60 m baseline means slope noise of several degrees (INFERENCE), and coverage ends at ±51.6° (SNIPPET).

## A set for 20 hours: five features in the MVP, three extras for the pitch

The **MVP** is A0 + A1 + A2 + A3/A4 + A5, i.e. the mask, the roughness spectrum at four baselines, the Wasserstein distance, closed depressions with drainage integration, and anisotropy. Total effort is about 10-14 person-hours, which with four people and about 20 clock hours until Sunday 15 Nov 2026 13:30 leaves half of the budget for validation, visualisation and a README with the citations required by the rules. This set coincides with the minimal specific set recommended in the notes on shape metrics (INFERENCE) and is anchored in NASA practice: the PGDA 90 product itself classifies the polar terrain with k-means on the roughness spectrum and summarises it by the mean standardised roughness (OPENED, [PGDA 90](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/90)).

The construction of the index should be transparent. Each feature is standardised by the mean and standard deviation of the **lunar reference population**, so that the distance means "how many lunar sigmas from the typical terrain of the target region"; slopes and roughnesses should be log-transformed first, and instead of four correlated levels one level and the ratios between baselines should be used as the shape of the spectrum (INFERENCE). The lunar observation that hectometre roughness correlates weakly with kilometre roughness, because different processes and ages are responsible (ABSTRACT ONLY, [Kreslavsky et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.icarus.2013.04.027)), is an argument that the shape of the spectrum carries information independent of the level. The result is given **per dimension** (roughness, slope distribution, topology, directionality), and only then as a sum with equal weights and a sensitivity analysis; this pattern is used by published scoring schemes (equal weights in Lambert and Reimold, OPENED, [doi:10.3389/fspas.2023.1186173](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspas.2023.1186173); stability of the winner under changes of weights and method in a TOPSIS analysis of 1247 points of the pole, ABSTRACT ONLY, [arXiv:2406.19863](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19863)). Target regions are best defined by the extents of the PGDA 78 rasters for the named sites (Connecting Ridge, Shackleton rim, Malapert, Nobile, Haworth), because NASA has not published the coordinates of the region centres (OPENED for the absence of coordinates in the 2022 release and in the LPSC 2026 abstract; choosing the rasters as the definition is INFERENCE).

Proposed division of work: the first person builds the reference population from PGDA 90 and runs the test of reproducing the NASA product (T1 below); the second moves the A1/A2 estimator to GLO-30 with the A0 mask; the third writes A3/A4/A5 on the common grid; the fourth assembles the validation set of known analogs, looks after citations and licences and prepares the visualisation. FABDEM is better skipped: its licence is given as CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 (SNIPPET, [GEE community catalog](https://gee-community-catalog.org/projects/fabdem/)), which conflicts with the openness requirement, and over bare ground it adds nothing anyway (INFERENCE).

**For the pitch** three elements with strong narrative power are worth adding. The first is the engineering fractions A7, because they translate similarity into mission language: the HLS vertical-orientation requirement is 0-8° (threshold) and 0-5° (goal) from the local vertical, quoted in a peer-reviewed paper (OPENED as a quote, [Kumari et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3847/PSJ/ac88c2); the HLS requirement documents themselves were not opened), a landing accuracy of 100 m used as a buffer (SNIPPET, [LPSC 2022 #1079](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2022/pdf/1079.pdf)), classes of 15° for walking EVA and 25° for a crewed rover (OPENED, [Kumari et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3847/PSJ/ac88c2)) and up to 20° on traverses between the landing zone and the habitat (OPENED, [NASA ACR24](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/acr24-lunar-mobility-drivers-and-needs.pdf)). The second is the lidar close-up A12 of Meteor Crater as proof that the team understands the limits of GLO-30. The numbers for this slide are, however, poorly documented: a diameter of about 1200 m, a depth of about 170 m and a rim of about 45 m come from a tertiary source (SNIPPET, [Wikipedia copy](https://ipfs.ssi.eecc.de/ipns/en.wikipedia-on-ipfs.org/wiki/Meteor_Crater)), and the lunar scaling of rim height of about 0.036 D and depth-to-diameter of about 0.2 is known from abstracts (SNIPPET, [Pike 1977](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1977iecp.symp..489P/abstract); SNIPPET, [LROC](https://lroc.im-ldi.com/images/975)); the resulting d/D ≈ 0.14 and a rim consistent with the scaling with too shallow a floor are the researchers' arithmetic (INFERENCE), better replaced by our own measurement from the 3DEP tile. The third, if there is time, is the shadow at low Sun A9: the 1.54° tilt of the lunar axis means that the Sun at the pole does not rise higher (OPENED, [Gläser et al. 2018](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20170007365/downloads/20170007365.pdf)), and one metre of obstacle then casts about 37 m of shadow (INFERENCE, arithmetic). The honest wording is "terrain whose shadow statistics at 1.5° and 5° match lunar terrain", not "a place with lunar illumination", because even at 80° latitude the summer noon Sun reaches about 33° (INFERENCE, textbook formula).

A mandatory element of the pitch is the "where the analogy breaks down" field for each proposed site, built on three levels: features matched and measured (topography, roughness, topology), matched partly (lithology, aridity, permafrost), and impossible to reproduce and therefore not scored. Preston and Dartnell state that for analog sites "there are no clearly defined and applied criteria" for assessment (OPENED, [Preston and Dartnell 2014](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/244C9EB88B74D6905133DD2228DD3112/core-reader)), and the CAFE study for ESA rated "scientific value and adequacy" by expert judgement on a 1-5 scale next to cost, permits, access and infrastructure (OPENED, [CAFE Executive Summary](https://esamultimedia.esa.int/docs/gsp/C4000104716ExS.pdf)). The Zodiak index thus replaces the first, subjective rating with a repeatable measurement and leaves logistics as a separate axis; in Foucher's vocabulary it measures a "planetary" and "engineering" analogy, not a petrological or astrobiological one.

## Six validation tests separate an index from a pretty map

The accuracy caveats should be listed in the README in the order in which they burden the result. First, the global Earth DEM is a surface model with a noise floor of 0.3-0.4° at 57 m (TEAM) and a relative specification of 2 m LE90 (OPENED), so all GLO-30 features hold from a baseline of about 200 m and only on bare ground. Second, the lunar grid is 25-85% interpolated (OPENED), and the kernel of the LDSM slope map is not described on the product page, so absolute LDSM values must not be compared with Earth slopes before the same kernel is applied to the LDEM. Third, the factor of 0.6-0.7 between the profile and the gridded statistic is a geometric expectation, not a measurement. Fourth, the table of discrimination strength and the choice of Wasserstein and Mahalanobis distance are methodological reasoning without validation in the literature. Fifth, illumination percentages are not comparable between papers: for the same Connecting Ridge point they are 64% (WAC images, 100 m), 76% (20 m DTM, ground level) and 89% (240 m DTM) (PARSED from a table in [Gläser et al. 2018](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20170007365/downloads/20170007365.pdf)). Sixth, numerical lunar slope medians for highlands and maria at 57-560 m were not taken from any open source (in the notes only RECALL, with diverging values), so they must be read from the PGDA 70 rasters, not quoted.

## The competition computes the same, and the Artemis status needs checking

Other Space Apps 2026 teams working on this challenge converge on a similar set. "Terrestrial Analog Finder" uses 12 x 12 km windows on a 30 m grid, the logarithm of local relief, the median and P90 of slope, short-baseline roughness, the hypsometric integral and the Wasserstein distance, scales differences by the interquartile range of the Earth pool, and admits itself that it takes geometry at one scale, that Copernicus heights include vegetation and that illumination is not represented (OPENED, [GitHub: terrestrial-analog-finder](https://github.com/shishir-kuet/terrestrial-analog-finder)). "ShadowAstra Atlas" rates about 80,200 cells of 0.5° with climate and terrain data, shows gravity, atmosphere and radiation "without scoring" and validates by hiding known sites; the results given there are numbers claimed by the authors, not independently checked (OPENED for the README, [GitHub: ShadowAstra_Atlas](https://github.com/ABRUBAB/ShadowAstra_Atlas)). Further repositories, which reportedly report among other things a mean ROC AUC of 0.79, are known only from search snippets (UNVERIFIED, [earthtwin](https://github.com/es-ratt/earthtwin), [twinworlds](https://github.com/JimmyGuajan/twinworlds), [mirror-terrain](https://github.com/talyamorgul/mirror-terrain)). It follows that relief, slope percentiles, hypsometry and Wasserstein are the baseline today, not a distinguishing feature. The open fields are: an estimator validated on a NASA product (T1), multiscale features with a reported ranking stability, topological and directional features, laser data on the Earth side, and a documented "where the analogy breaks down" field. In the peer-reviewed literature no global, DEM-based, multi-criteria analog search for lunar sites was found; this is a "not found" result, not proof of absence, and should be worded as such.

The official challenge page could not be opened (TLS certificate error on every attempt), so the organiser's list of "considerations" is unverified; only a summary passed on by third parties is available, speaking of using open Earth, Moon and Mars data to "identify and characterize new" analogs (SECONDARY, [HackAthlone](https://hackathlone.com/challenges); official address: [Space Apps 2026](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/2026/challenges/permanent-moon-base-locations-and-mars/)). Someone in the team should open this page in a browser and match the names of the criteria to its literal wording.

The status of the Artemis programme **needs confirmation**. What is certain: in August 2022 NASA identified 13 regions of about 15 x 15 km, all within 6° of latitude of the pole (OPENED, [NASA release of 19 Aug 2022](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-identifies-candidate-regions-for-landing-next-americans-on-Moon/)), and an LPSC 2026 abstract lists 9 regions: Peak near Cabeus B, Haworth, Malapert Massif, Mons Mouton Plateau, Mons Mouton, Nobile Rim 1, Nobile Rim 2, de Gerlache Rim 2 and Slater Plain (OPENED, [NTRS 20250011660](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20250011660)). According to secondary sources, at the end of February 2026 NASA restructured the programme: Artemis III is to be a docking test in low Earth orbit in 2027, the first crewed landing moves to Artemis IV with a target of early 2028, and Gateway is described as cancelled (SNIPPET, [SpacePolicyOnline](https://spacepolicyonline.com/news/nasa-makes-a-course-correction-for-the-artemis-program/); OPENED, but a tertiary source, [Wikipedia: Artemis IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis_IV)). NASA's own 2026 release was not opened, and the LPSC 2026 abstract still describes Artemis III as a landing, i.e. it was most likely written before the change. The safe wording for the pitch is "candidate regions of the Artemis III era at the south pole", without assigning them to a specific mission and without dates.

## Conclusions

The team's pilot and the review of the notes lead to the same place: the value of the project lies not in the number of features, but in the symmetry of the measurement. Every feature that can be computed with one code on the LDEM and on an Earth DEM at a baseline above the noise floor of both sources is worth more than ten layers in which the Moon and Earth are described by different methods. Test T1, reproducing a ready NASA map with our own code, turns the claim "we compare quantitatively" into something the jury can check, and none of the competing teams reviewed describes such a step.

The second consequence is less comfortable: at 30 m globally, Earth is measured worse for this task than the lunar pole, and the relation reverses only where airborne lidar exists. The global index therefore points to candidates, and only a lidar close-up or an ICESat-2 profile confirms them at hectometre baselines. Designing the product as a two-stage one (GLO-30 scan from 200 m, then laser verification of the short list) turns the data limitation into an element of the method and gives a natural answer to the question of further development after the hackathon.
