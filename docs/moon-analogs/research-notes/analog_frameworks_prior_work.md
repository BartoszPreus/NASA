## 1. Formal criteria frameworks: ESA CAFE study, the Catalogue of Planetary Analogues, and Preston & Dartnell 2014

### Takeaway
The only agency-level, expert-elicited criteria set found is ESA's CAFE study (Open University, 2012): five sequential criteria (scientific merit and relevance to Moon/Mars, then cost, permissions, physical access, human infrastructure) scored 1 to 5, plus five secondary factors. Preston & Dartnell (2014) state explicitly that there are "no clearly defined and followed criteria" for evaluating analog sites, which is the gap a quantitative index can claim to address.

### Cited Findings

CAFE study (Executive Summary Report, Issue 1, ESA Contract 4000104716/11/NL/AF; authors Louisa Preston, Simeon Barber, Monica Grady, Open University, with ALTEC SpA and Magna Parva Ltd):
- Purpose: "preparing a complete catalogue of terrestrial analogue environments that are appropriate for testing human space exploration-related scientific field activities." — [CAFE Executive Summary, ESA](https://esamultimedia.esa.int/docs/gsp/C4000104716ExS.pdf)
- Founding caveat, quotable: "As no single analogue can provide an identical representation of landforms, materials and processes found on the Moon and Mars, it is necessary to select those analogues that best represent particular regions or processes of interest." — [CAFE Executive Summary](https://esamultimedia.esa.int/docs/gsp/C4000104716ExS.pdf)
- Criteria were produced at an expert meeting covering planetary geology, astrobiology, technology, human factors and outreach; the criteria "were discussed and rated on importance." — [CAFE Executive Summary](https://esamultimedia.esa.int/docs/gsp/C4000104716ExS.pdf)
- Five SEQUENTIAL (primary) requirements, in order: (1) Scientific Merit, "the number one priority ... is scientific merit and relevance for the Moon and/or Mars"; (2) Cost; (3) Accessibility (permissions); (4) Accessibility (physical); (5) Human Infrastructure Capabilities. — [CAFE Executive Summary, section 3 and figure](https://esamultimedia.esa.int/docs/gsp/C4000104716ExS.pdf)
- Five ADDITIONAL (secondary) requirements, to be included in any decision but which "can be worked around if time and money allow": Climate, Public Engagement, Human Factors, Availability of Analogue Materials, Communication and Data Transfer. — [CAFE Executive Summary, figure "Requirements for choosing an Analogue Site"](https://esamultimedia.esa.int/docs/gsp/C4000104716ExS.pdf)
- Scoring scale actually used: sites "were judged on their scientific merit, the cost of conducting fieldwork at them, the level of permissions needed, the ease or difficulty of accessibility, and the level of human infrastructure available or needed. These were ranked on a scale of 1 to 5 with 1 being the least scientifically relevant/hardest to get to/most expensive to work at; and 5 the best/easiest/cheapest. The top 10 analogue sites were chosen." — [CAFE Executive Summary, section 6](https://esamultimedia.esa.int/docs/gsp/C4000104716ExS.pdf)
- No weights are stated; the ordering is lexicographic ("sequential"), not a weighted sum. The top-10 list itself is not given in the executive summary. — [CAFE Executive Summary](https://esamultimedia.esa.int/docs/gsp/C4000104716ExS.pdf)
- Lunar-relevant target categories defined by CAFE: "On the Moon, important targets range from the ubiquitous presence of impact craters and their associated features, to volcanic constructs and terrains, granular flows and geological materials in the form of anorthosite and basaltic rocks. Locations that display evidence of frozen water and volatiles are also considered." — [CAFE Executive Summary, section 4.1](https://esamultimedia.esa.int/docs/gsp/C4000104716ExS.pdf)
- Mars target categories: impact features, volcanic landscapes, fluvial features, aeolian processes, ice-related geomorphology, subsurface water deposits, plus astrobiological potential and minerals. — [CAFE Executive Summary, section 4.1](https://esamultimedia.esa.int/docs/gsp/C4000104716ExS.pdf)
- Classes of surface activity an analog must support: robotic surveys, manned survey/human sortie, human in-situ analysis, human sample return, short traverse, long traverse, post-human robotic studies. — [CAFE Executive Summary, section 4.2](https://esamultimedia.esa.int/docs/gsp/C4000104716ExS.pdf)
- Catalogue content per site: scientific details plus "information on location; environment; infrastructure available and needed for mission success; and key references." Size: "around 90 sites." — [CAFE Executive Summary, section 5](https://esamultimedia.esa.int/docs/gsp/C4000104716ExS.pdf)
- Campaign-design recommendations with usable numbers: sites "shall contain more than one geologic unit"; "One campaign should be based in a 1 km area utilising single traverses, the other in a 20 km area to allow for multiple traverses"; one site should mimic "physical and chemical features found on the Moon" and another the physical, chemical and astrobiological features of Mars; at least one site in a remote, extreme environment. — [CAFE Executive Summary, section 6](https://esamultimedia.esa.int/docs/gsp/C4000104716ExS.pdf)
- Outcome: Teide National Park (Tenerife) chosen as the LUNAR analog campaign concept (volcanic geology, "lunar-like palaeoregolith and soils", traverses around Llano de Ucanca); Axel Heiberg Island (McGill Arctic Research Station) as the Mars concept. — [CAFE Executive Summary, sections 6.1-6.2](https://esamultimedia.esa.int/docs/gsp/C4000104716ExS.pdf)
- ESA's public description (17 Oct 2013): the catalogue covers "all seven continents", records practical details such as local infrastructure, legal requirements, a local contact, nearest petrol station and dangerous wildlife; categories named are impact craters, lava flows, deserts and tundra; method described as first identifying interesting areas on Mars and the Moon, then finding the nearest Earth analogue for each. — [ESA, Cataloguing otherworldly landscapes on Earth](https://www.esa.int/Enabling_Support/Preparing_for_the_Future/Discovery_and_Preparation/Cataloguing_otherworldly_landscapes_on_Earth)
- Full catalogue PDF location (not readable by my tools, file over 10 MB): [The Catalogue of Planetary Analogues, ESA](https://esamultimedia.esa.int/docs/gsp/The_Catalogue_of_Planetary_Analogues.pdf). Formal citation as given by Foucher et al. 2021: "Preston, L., Grady, M., Barber, S., 2012. CAFE - Concepts for Activities in the Field for Exploration, TN2: the Catalogue of Planetary Analogues. The Open University." — [Foucher et al. 2021 reference list](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pss.2021.105162)
- The originally supplied Nebula URL now returns HTTP 301 to activities.esa.int; the working copy is the esamultimedia.esa.int link above. — [nebula.esa.int link (redirects)](https://nebula.esa.int/sites/default/files/neb_tec_study/1115/C4000104716ExS.pdf)

Preston & Dartnell 2014 (Int. J. Astrobiology 13(1), 81-98):
- States that for analogue sites "there are no clearly defined and followed criteria with which to evaluate them." — [Preston & Dartnell 2014, Cambridge core-reader](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/244C9EB88B74D6905133DD2228DD3112/core-reader)
- States "no analogue site is a perfect representation of another planet or moon." — [Preston & Dartnell 2014](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/244C9EB88B74D6905133DD2228DD3112/core-reader)
- Aim: for each site, summarise the physico-chemical environmental features and "critically assess the fidelity with which they emulate their intended target locale"; also notes analog value does not depend on fidelity alone, accessibility is a separate factor. — [Preston & Dartnell 2014](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/244C9EB88B74D6905133DD2228DD3112/core-reader)
- Three-tier evidence classification (after Soare et al.): first-order analogues rest on direct empirical evidence (e.g., terrestrial volcanoes and impact craters versus confirmed Martian ones); second-order on indirect evidence awaiting confirmation (e.g., Lake Vostok for a Europan ocean); third-order "are those that are not sustained by direct or indirect target evidence" (e.g., extremophiles). — [Preston & Dartnell 2014](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/244C9EB88B74D6905133DD2228DD3112/core-reader)
- Recommendation: "This review thus highlights the need for a global open access database for planetary analogues." — [Preston & Dartnell 2014](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/244C9EB88B74D6905133DD2228DD3112/core-reader)
- Mars sites grouped by epoch (from figure captions): Early Mars: Pilbara stromatolites, Rio Tinto, Golden Deposit, Grand Prismatic Spring, Haughton, Dongwanzi Ophiolite. Middle Mars: Axel Heiberg Island, Beacon Valley, Fimmvörðuháls, Koriaksky volcano, Bockfjord Volcanic Complex, Mount Kilimanjaro. Present Mars: Atacama, Antarctic Dry Valleys, Mojave, Namib, Kess Kess mounds, Qaidam Basin. — [Preston & Dartnell 2014](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/244C9EB88B74D6905133DD2228DD3112/core-reader)
- The review covers 33 analogue sites across Venus, Mars, Titan, Enceladus, Europa etc. **[UNVERIFIED, search snippet of the ADS abstract]** — [ADS record](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014IJAsB..13...81P)
- The review notes the CAFE catalogue documents Mars and Moon analogues but was not publicly available at the time. — [Preston & Dartnell 2014](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/244C9EB88B74D6905133DD2228DD3112/core-reader)

### Inferences
- CAFE's criteria split cleanly into one "similarity" criterion (scientific merit and relevance) and four "logistics" criteria. A DEM-based similarity index quantifies only the first one, which CAFE left as an expert 1 to 5 judgement. The team can therefore say: "we replace the subjective scientific-relevance score with a reproducible measurement, and keep CAFE's logistics criteria as separate, optional layers."
- CAFE's 1 km and 20 km campaign footprints give a defensible, citable pair of analysis window sizes.
- The lunar target list in CAFE (impact craters, volcanic terrains, granular flows, anorthosite, basalt, frozen volatiles) is an established checklist that can be mapped one-to-one onto index dimensions (crater morphology, volcanic landform class, slope/roughness, lithology, cold-trap proxy).
- Because CAFE's ordering is lexicographic, a weighted-sum index is a methodological departure and should be stated as such.

### Gaps
- The per-site 1 to 5 scores and the top-10 list from CAFE were not found (they are probably in the full Technical Notes, not in the executive summary).
- The full Catalogue PDF could not be parsed, so per-site coordinates and the lunar/Mars flags from the catalogue itself are missing. The site names below (section 4) come from Table 1 of the executive summary only.
- The second half of the Preston & Dartnell full text (after character 100,000) was not read; a site table with locations may be there.
- The IMEWG analog site database was not located at all; I found no reliable source on it.

## 2. Fidelity concepts and analog typologies

### Takeaway
The literature converges on five analog types (planetary/geomorphological, process, petrological/mineralogical, astrobiological, engineering), each with a named, published "analogy limitation". Foucher et al. (2021) argue that analogy must be defined per property and per use ("functional analogue"), which directly supports a multi-criteria index reported per dimension instead of one opaque score.

### Cited Findings

Foucher et al. 2021 (Planetary and Space Science 197, 105162; open access, CC BY; full text read):
- Definition: "Functional analogues are defined as terrestrial sites, materials or objects exhibiting general properties more or less similar to those anticipated on the targeted extraterrestrial body, but having specific analogue properties that are highly or perfectly relevant for a given use." — [Foucher et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pss.2021.105162) ([Kent repository copy](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/109579/))
- Key principle: "it is, thus, important to focus on the specific properties that need to be imitated and to consider analogue properties rather than analogue sites or samples alone." — [Foucher et al. 2021, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pss.2021.105162)
- "By definition, the perfect analogue that matches or mimics all properties of an extra-terrestrial site or sample does not exist." — [Foucher et al. 2021, section 3.2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pss.2021.105162)
- Five categories of analogue SITES: "i) Sites used for their planetary analogy; ii) Sites used for analogies in their mechanical and chemical processes; iii) Sites used for their petrological and mineralogical analogy; iv) Analogue sites of astrobiological interest; v) Engineering analogue sites." — [Foucher et al. 2021, section 2.1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pss.2021.105162)
- Fig. 1 summary table (relevance / use / analogy limitation), transcribed:

| Type | Relevance | Use | Analogy limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Planetary | Large scale geological structures, geomorphology, landscape | Explain/illustrate extraterrestrial geological structures; astronaut training (scientific) | Environmental conditions, lithology |
| Process | Mechanical and/or chemical alteration, transportation, hydrothermalism, impact, metamorphism, weathering | Explain/illustrate extraterrestrial geological processes | Origin of the processes, environmental conditions, petrography |
| Petrology | Mineral formation | Collection of minerals | Formation processes, geological setting |
| Astrobiology | Extinct or extant life in analogue environmental conditions | Study metabolism; search for biosignatures | Speculative analogy with extraterrestrial life |
| Engineering | Topography, mechanical and physical properties of the surface, environmental conditions, facilities | Rover and instrument testing, astronaut training (practical) | Nature of the obstacles, environmental conditions |

  — [Foucher et al. 2021, Fig. 1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pss.2021.105162)
- Scale of planetary analogue sites: "landscapes or outcrops that may range from a few metres up to several hundred kilometres in size." Landform classes listed: impact craters (simple to complex), desert landforms (erg, serir, inselbergs, salt lakes, playas), volcanic (volcanoes, volcanic fields, lava tubes), hydrothermal, glacial and periglacial. — [Foucher et al. 2021, section 2.1.1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pss.2021.105162)
- Engineering sites: composition may be irrelevant; "in a rover ground clearance test, the composition of obstacles is not directly relevant, only their height and size. Surfaces with specific mechanical properties (roughness, adhesion, etc.) or cohesion (sand, gravel, etc.) may be also relevant." — [Foucher et al. 2021, section 2.1.5](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pss.2021.105162)
- Lunar-specific statements: anorthosite is "present in high proportions compared to Earth (e.g. anorthosite on the Moon)" so "few areas on the Earth's surface are representative"; named lunar petrological analogs are the Stillwater complex, Montana (Salpas et al. 1983, "Stillwater anorthosites: a lunar analog?") and the Vredefort dome, South Africa (lunar metamorphism analogue, Gibson et al. 2002); Hawaiian lava flows were used to constrain thickness of lunar mare flows seen by LROC NAC (Rumpf et al. 2020). Note: the paper's sentence reads "anorthosites analogous to lunar basalts", which looks like a slip for lunar highland rocks; quote with care. — [Foucher et al. 2021, sections 2.1.2-2.1.3](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pss.2021.105162)
- Selection method: a decision-tree ("logical pathways", Fig. 4) starting from "What do you want to study?" and branching on technical vs scientific output, scale (site vs sample), environmental conditions (space vs terrestrial), "Can you make a fieldtrip?", ending in one of the analog types or a "dead end". Practical considerations named: "time, money, facilities and analogue availability (e.g. location and abundance)". — [Foucher et al. 2021, section 3.2 and Fig. 4](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pss.2021.105162)
- The paper also weighs "environmental and financial considerations, such as limiting ... non-essential travel to exotic terrestrial analogue sites." — [Foucher et al. 2021, Introduction](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pss.2021.105162)
- Compendia of sites it points to: Garry & Bleacher (2011), GSA Special Paper 483 "Analogs for Planetary Exploration", and Preston et al. (2012). — [Foucher et al. 2021, section 2.1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pss.2021.105162)

Osinski, Léveillé, Berinstain, Lebeuf & Bamsey 2006 (Geoscience Canada 33(4), 175-188):
- Definition: terrestrial analogues are "places on Earth that approximate, in some respect, the geological, environmental and putative biological conditions" of another body, now or in the past. — [Osinski et al. 2006, UNB journal copy](https://journals.lib.unb.ca/index.php/GC/article/download/10224/10596?inline=1)
- Three areas of analogue work: comparative planetary geology; astrobiology; exploration science (instrument testing, astronaut training, robot-human interaction, mission control, telemedicine, communications). — [Osinski et al. 2006](https://journals.lib.unb.ca/index.php/GC/article/download/10224/10596?inline=1)
- "Fidelity is considered to be a key element in any analogue activity, particularly exploration-related activities." Sites in its Table 1 are ranked by fidelity; fidelity needed for a rover test depends on topography, temperature, precipitation and rock abundance. — [Osinski et al. 2006](https://journals.lib.unb.ca/index.php/GC/article/download/10224/10596?inline=1)

Other fidelity schemes:
- Wikipedia's "Terrestrial analogue site" article classifies fidelity on four axes: geomorphology, geochemistry, exobiology, exploration conditions. The article gives no source for the scheme and no numeric ratings (cells are descriptive text). **[SECONDARY]** — [Wikipedia, Terrestrial analogue site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrestrial_analogue_site)
- A 2004 NASA paper is reported to classify analogue missions by degree of fidelity across ten key areas. **[UNVERIFIED, search snippet; not opened]** — [NASA NTRS 20040061968](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20040061968)
- Lee et al. (2010) are reported to argue that the most important consideration in using an analog site is its fidelity for the usage sought. **[UNVERIFIED, search snippet]** — [ADS record of Preston & Dartnell, where the snippet appeared](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014IJAsB..13...81P)
- ten Kate & Preston's "Earth Analogues" chapter is reported to state that most lunar analogue sites were chosen to test mission concepts and instruments, not to study lunar rocks. **[UNVERIFIED, search snippet]** — [Earth Analogues chapter, ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/332752844_Earth_Analogues/fulltext/636686a254eb5f547ca6da52/Earth-Analogues.pdf)

### Inferences
- A DEM-based index is, in Foucher's vocabulary, a measure of "planetary analogy" (geomorphology) and of "engineering analogy" (topography, obstacles, roughness). It is NOT a petrological, process or astrobiological analogy measure unless lithology and climate layers are added. Using these exact category names in the report prevents over-claiming.
- Foucher's Fig. 1 "analogy limitation" column is a ready-made template for a "where the analogy breaks" field per result.
- Osinski's fidelity drivers for rover tests (topography, temperature, precipitation, rock abundance) map onto four obtainable global layers: DEM derivatives, land surface temperature, precipitation/aridity, and a rock-abundance or bare-ground proxy.

### Gaps
- Léveillé 2010 was not located or opened; only Léveillé 2009 (C. R. Palevol 8, 637-648, "Validation of astrobiology technologies and instrument operations in terrestrial analogue environments") appears, via the Foucher reference list.
- Osinski et al. 2006 Table 1 fidelity rankings (the actual category labels and values) were not extracted.
- No official NASA analog-missions taxonomy document was opened; the NASA item above is snippet-only.

## 3. Scoring schemes with numbers (impact-site ranking, MCDA/TOPSIS)

### Takeaway
Two published, numeric, reproducible schemes exist to mirror: Lambert & Reimold (2023) score 11 impact structures on equally weighted practical and geological criteria (Ries first), and Peña-Asensio et al. (2024) apply GIS plus TOPSIS to 1,247 lunar south-pole locations with a sensitivity analysis over weights and methods. Neither scores terrain similarity between Earth and the Moon.

### Cited Findings

Lambert & Reimold 2023, "Terrestrial impact sites as field analogs for planetary exploration", Front. Astron. Space Sci. 10, published 30 Oct 2023 (full text read in two segments, first 200,000 of 231,460 characters):
- Sites: 11 structures assessed (12 listed; Clearwater East excluded): Araguainha, Clearwater West, Haughton, Meteor Crater, Mistastin, Ries, Rochechouart, Steinheim, Sudbury, Talemzane, Vredefort. — [Lambert & Reimold 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspas.2023.1186173)
- Practical criteria: Time factor (hours from nearest major international airport to site, and to that airport from the start point); Cost factor (euros for the same two legs); Other limiting factors (political, sanitary, administrative, environmental, climatic risk, each 0 = none to 4 = very high, climatic risk scored by months of inaccessibility); Benefit factor (living conditions 0 to 3; working conditions: museum, sample library, drill cores, boreholes, facilities). — [Lambert & Reimold 2023, section 3](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspas.2023.1186173)
- Geological criteria: Crater Preservation CP, 0 to 5 (5 = initial topography and ejecta intact, 0 = buried); Outcrop Situation OS = outcrop quality (1-3) + what can be covered in two field days (1-3); Geological Features and Lithologies GFL = 20 impact features/lithologies (IFL) + 11 target features/lithologies (TFL), each 0 (absent) to 3 (high). — [Lambert & Reimold 2023, Table 2](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspas.2023.1186173)
- Weights: the three geological factors are equally weighted; the four practical factors are equally weighted; practical and geological blocks are equally weighted in the overall score. — [Lambert & Reimold 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspas.2023.1186173)
- Table 2 marks (CP / OS / IFL / TFL / GFL): Araguainha 3/5/24/11/35; Clearwater West 3/4/16/6/22; Haughton 3/3/13/5/18; Meteor Crater 5/4/10/5/15; Mistastin 3/4/19/8/27; Ries 4/5/19/10/29; Rochechouart 2/4/27/13/40; Steinheim 3/3/11/4/15; Sudbury 2/4/20/10/30; Talemzane 4/4/10/3/13; Vredefort 1/4/15/16/31. — [Lambert & Reimold 2023, Table 2](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspas.2023.1186173)
- Overall ranking: 1 Ries; 2 Rochechouart (13% below Ries); 3 Steinheim; 4 Vredefort; Araguainha, Meteor Crater and Sudbury 31-34% below the leader; Talemzane 41% below; the three Northern Canada sites (Clearwater West, Haughton, Mistastin) 60-67% below Ries. Authors recommend a combined Ries-Rochechouart-Steinheim expedition. — [Lambert & Reimold 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspas.2023.1186173)
- Conflict to flag: the abstract-level summary says Rochechouart scores highest on geological criteria, while the results text (second segment) says Ries leads the geological score with Araguainha 2% behind. Both come from the same paper as summarised by the fetch tool; the per-factor table above is the safer thing to cite, and the exact normalised geological ranking should be re-checked in the paper before quoting. — [Lambert & Reimold 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspas.2023.1186173)
- Stated sensitivity: rankings depend on chosen time/cost thresholds and on the two-day fieldwork limit; the CP scale "may lack sensitivity"; CO2 footprint of travel not included. — [Lambert & Reimold 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspas.2023.1186173)
- Coordinates as printed in its Table 1 are reproduced in section 4 below.

Peña-Asensio, Neira-Acosta & Sánchez-Lozano 2024, "Evaluating potential landing sites for the Artemis III mission using a multi-criteria decision making approach" (arXiv:2406.19863; accepted in Acta Astronautica) **[ABSTRACT ONLY]**:
- Method: GIS combined with MCDM, technique TOPSIS. — [arXiv:2406.19863](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19863)
- Criteria: surface visibility, HLS-astronaut line of sight, Permanently Shadowed Regions, sunlight exposure, direct communication with Earth, geological units, mafic mineral abundance. — [arXiv:2406.19863](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19863)
- Scale: 1,247 locations meeting Human Landing System requirements, within the 13 candidate regions plus "Site 004" near the lunar south pole. — [arXiv:2406.19863](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19863)
- Result: best location in Site DM2 (Nobile Rim 2), about 84.20° S, 60.70° E (84°12'5.61" S, 60°41'59.61" E in the abstract). Sensitivity analysis: the top site stays the same under different MCDM methods and criteria weightings (including weightings prioritising illumination and PSRs). — [arXiv:2406.19863](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19863)

CAFE numeric scheme (repeated here for completeness): five criteria, 1 to 5 each, top 10 chosen, sequential priority. — [CAFE Executive Summary](https://esamultimedia.esa.int/docs/gsp/C4000104716ExS.pdf)

### Inferences
- The defensible pattern across all three schemes is: (a) separate "similarity/science" from "logistics", (b) equal weights by default, (c) publish a sensitivity analysis showing rank stability under weight changes. Peña-Asensio's criteria also provide the lunar-side "target description" (illumination, PSRs, Earth visibility, geological unit) for the Artemis sites the challenge refers to.
- Lambert & Reimold penalise Haughton and Mistastin almost entirely on logistics, not geology. A terrain-similarity index and a logistics score can therefore disagree strongly, and the report should present them as two axes.
- Lambert & Reimold's numbers can serve as a small external validation set for the impact-crater dimension (e.g., CP score versus a DEM crater-preservation measure for the 11 sites).

### Gaps
- I found no published application of AHP or TOPSIS to terrestrial ANALOG site selection specifically; the only MCDA example located is for lunar landing sites.
- The Peña-Asensio criteria weights and the alternative MCDM methods tested are in the full paper, not the abstract.
- The last ~31,000 characters of Lambert & Reimold (conclusions, supplementary notes) were not read.

- A practical "known analogs" validation/exclusion list of roughly 35 points with sourced coordinates can be built immediately from tables B and C plus SP Crater, Mistastin, Expedition Fiord and (after verification) Kilbourne Hole. Etna, CHILL-ICE (Iceland lava tubes), Askja, SP Crater/Black Point, Cinder Lake, Kilbourne Hole, Lofoten, Stillwater.
- Known analogs are selected for very different reasons (lithology, impact process, isolation, logistics). Sites such as NEEMO, Concordia, LunAres, SAM/Biosphere 2, Marseille Bay and HI-SEAS's habitat are OPERATIONAL analogs; a terrain index should not be expected to rank them highly, and they should be removed from a terrain validation set.
- Cinder Lake is an artificial 1:1 replica of a mare surface, so it is a unique positive control at the scale of tens to hundreds of metres, below the resolution of a 30 m global DEM.
- The Wikipedia coordinates for areal sites (Atacama, Svalbard, Antarctic Dry Valleys) are single centroid points for regions hundreds of kilometres across and should be treated as region labels, not point targets.

## 5. Prior quantitative or automated analog identification and Earth-Moon/Mars terrain similarity work

### Takeaway
Peer-reviewed quantitative work compares planetary roughness statistics (Kreslavsky & Head; Aharonson et al.; Rosenburg et al.) but does not search Earth for analogs. Automated analog search exists only as regional, image-based or non-peer-reviewed work (Meng et al. Qaidam CNN; GEAR for a trench analog; Sanganeria's 10-image texture ranking; a withdrawn 2013 EGU abstract), plus 2026 Space Apps team repositories. I found no peer-reviewed, global, DEM-based, multi-criteria Earth analog search for lunar sites.

### Cited Findings

Sanganeria 2026, "Finding Earth's Best Testbeds for Moon and Mars Rovers: A Quantitative Terrain Comparison", National High School Journal of Science (NHSJS Reports), 21 April 2026 (full page read):
- Data: ten grayscale orbital/satellite images per body (Earth, Moon, Mars) from public archives; per-image provenance (sensor, date, resolution) not provided. — [Sanganeria 2026, NHSJS](https://nhsjs.com/2026/finding-earths-best-testbeds-for-moon-and-mars-rovers-a-quantitative-terrain-comparison/)
- Ten Earth sites: Askja Caldera; North Atacama; South Atacama; Devon Island; Mauna Kea; Meteor Crater; Nevada Desert; Nevada Test Site; Utah Desert; Wadi Rum. — [Sanganeria 2026](https://nhsjs.com/2026/finding-earths-best-testbeds-for-moon-and-mars-rovers-a-quantitative-terrain-comparison/)
- Method: min-max normalised intensity; 4-D feature vector (mean and standard deviation of intensity; mean and standard deviation of a local-intensity-difference "roughness proxy"); Euclidean distance to the mean lunar and Martian centroids; leave-one-reference-out resampling as a sensitivity check (stability numbers not reported). — [Sanganeria 2026](https://nhsjs.com/2026/finding-earths-best-testbeds-for-moon-and-mars-rovers-a-quantitative-terrain-comparison/)
- Results, Moon distance (rank) / Mars distance (rank): Nevada Desert 0.022 (1=) / 0.016 (1); Askja 0.022 (1=) / 0.034 (6); Mauna Kea 0.028 (3) / 0.050 (9); Wadi Rum 0.033 (4) / 0.022 (2); Nevada Test Site 0.038 (5) / 0.023 (3); Atacama image 1 0.041 (6) / 0.025 (4=); Meteor Crater 0.042 (7) / 0.025 (4=); Utah Desert 0.070 (8) / 0.038 (7=); Atacama image 2 0.080 (9) / 0.038 (7=); Devon Island 0.186 (10) / 0.060 (10). — [Sanganeria 2026, Table 2](https://nhsjs.com/2026/finding-earths-best-testbeds-for-moon-and-mars-rovers-a-quantitative-terrain-comparison/)
- Stated limitations: descriptors are image texture, "not physical elevation, slope, or mechanical roughness"; illumination geometry, albedo and sensor processing can dominate; resolution dependence; only ten images per body; no gravity, regolith or wheel-soil modelling; not validated against DEM-derived roughness or field data; "not a substitute for physics-based terrain assessment." — [Sanganeria 2026](https://nhsjs.com/2026/finding-earths-best-testbeds-for-moon-and-mars-rovers-a-quantitative-terrain-comparison/)
- Internal inconsistencies noticed on reading: the abstract says established analogs "including Devon Island" are among the closest matches, but Table 2 ranks Devon Island last for both bodies; a "Similarity Score" formula uses elevation terms that the method does not compute; figure captions label intensity as altitude in metres. — [Sanganeria 2026](https://nhsjs.com/2026/finding-earths-best-testbeds-for-moon-and-mars-rovers-a-quantitative-terrain-comparison/)

Meng et al., "Deep learning selection of analogues for Mars landing sites in the Qaidam Basin, Qinghai-Tibet Plateau", arXiv:2501.08584 (submitted 31 Dec 2024; 22 pages, 16 figures) **[ABSTRACT ONLY]**:
- Authors: Fanwei Meng, Xiaopeng Wang, André Antunes, Jie Zhao, Guoliang Zhou, Biqiong Wu, Tianqi Hao. Method: a CNN trained with a discriminative loss to learn a similarity metric between Earth and Mars surface images; applied to a small evaporite area of the Qaidam Basin; intended use is simulated selection of priority Mars landing sites. The abstract reports no accuracy figures, dataset size or resolution. — [arXiv:2501.08584](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.08584)
- A search summary describes it as a Siamese CNN with Euclidean distance between feature vectors and a threshold of 0.5 for "very high similarity". **[UNVERIFIED, search snippet of the PDF]** — [arXiv:2501.08584 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.08584)

Liu et al. 2026, "GEAR: Geography-knowledge Enhanced Analog Recognition Framework in Extreme Environments", arXiv:2603.18626 (19 March 2026, cs.CV) **[ABSTRACT ONLY]**:
- Important scope note: GEAR searches for terrestrial analogs of the MARIANA TRENCH on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, not for Moon or Mars analogs. It is relevant as a method template only. — [arXiv:2603.18626](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18626)
- Method: three stages: (1) skeleton-guided screening and clipping of candidate valleys by size and linear morphology; (2) physics-aware filtering with a Topographic Waveform Comparator and a Morphological Texture Module; (3) MSG-Net, a morphology-integrated Siamese graph network using geomorphological metrics. Scale: about 2.5 million km². Result: F1 1.38 percentage points above the best baseline; extracted features correlate with biological data. Releases an expert-annotated topographic similarity dataset. — [arXiv:2603.18626](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18626)

Milenković, Székely & Pfeifer 2013, "Roughness Analysis of Martian Landforms and its Analogues on Earth, Moon and Other Solar System Bodies", Geophysical Research Abstracts 15, EGU2013-11983 (abstract PDF read in full):
- Data: DTMs from photogrammetry (HRSC), laser altimetry (MOLA) and radar (SRTM). Mars landform patterns are the starting set; analogues searched on other bodies by feature content, "but not necessary at similar scales." Analogue sets: (a) canyon banks, (b) Martian chaos, (c) erosion features, (d) glacial features, (e) lava flow features. — [EGU2013-11983](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU2013/EGU2013-11983.pdf)
- Method: three levels. Level 1: overall roughness per landscape by fitting a power law to roughness indices (standard deviation of elevation, topographic prominence, openness) across scales, i.e. a fractal dimension per index. Level 2: variogram and correlation length at the important scales. Level 3: roughness pattern relative to local topographic trend. No results are given in the abstract. — [EGU2013-11983](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU2013/EGU2013-11983.pdf)
- The EGU programme lists the contribution as "(withdrawn)"; no follow-up paper was found. **[UNVERIFIED, search snippet of the session listing]** — [EGU2013 session listing](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU2013/orals/11707)

- Other team repositories report: same-resolution comparison (40 m Moon; 200 m and 463 m Mars), relief measured as P95-P5 elevation within 15 km on a 2 km grid across LOLA/MOLA/Copernicus, validation "Mean ROC AUC 0.79 (23 targets)", and checks that known analogs rank above vegetated control sites. **[UNVERIFIED, search snippets; repositories not opened individually]** — [GitHub, es-ratt/earthtwin](https://github.com/es-ratt/earthtwin); [GitHub, JimmyGuajan/twinworlds](https://github.com/JimmyGuajan/twinworlds); [GitHub, talyamorgul/mirror-terrain](https://github.com/talyamorgul/mirror-terrain)

### Inferences
- State of the art, honestly summarised: (1) cross-body roughness statistics are well established for Mars and the Moon separately; (2) analog identification is still expert-driven; (3) automated attempts are image-based (texture or CNN) and regional. A global, DEM-based, multi-criteria index with validation against a known-analog list is not something I could find in the peer-reviewed literature. This is "not found", not "proven absent".
- Sanganeria's own limitation list is effectively a specification for a better method: use elevation and slope instead of image intensity, control for illumination and resolution, state provenance per tile, validate against known sites. Devon Island ranking last in an image-texture method is a concrete illustration of why DEM-based metrics are needed.
- Aharonson's finding that Mars has a longer slope tail than Earth because erosion (planation) is weaker gives a physical reason why hyper-arid, low-erosion terrestrial terrains should score best, and why slope-distribution SHAPE (not only the median) is a meaningful criterion.
- Rosenburg's baseline range (about 17 m to 2.7 km) is mostly below 30 m DEM resolution at the short end, so only the upper part of the lunar roughness scale range (roughly 60 m to a few km) is comparable with Copernicus GLO-30.
- The competing Space Apps teams converge on similar feature sets (relief, slope percentiles, roughness, hypsometry, slope-distribution distance) and on leave-known-sites-out validation. Differentiators left open: multi-scale rank stability, explicit use of published criteria frameworks (CAFE, Foucher), and a documented "where the analogy breaks" field.

### Gaps
- No "Earth Similarity Index"-style or "Mars-likeness" global index for terrestrial terrain was found; the extended search returned none. Likewise no Atacama-Mars or Antarctic Dry Valleys-Mars climate similarity map was located.
- Full texts of Meng et al., GEAR, Kreslavsky & Head 1999, Aharonson et al. 2001 and the Rosenburg thesis were not read; numbers beyond the abstracts are unverified.
- Whether Milenković et al. ever published results is unknown.
- I did not find a peer-reviewed comparison of Hurst exponents across Earth, Moon and Mars with numeric values.

## 7. Recognised limits of terrestrial analogs and how frameworks document "where the analogy breaks"

### Takeaway
Published frameworks treat the limits as inherent and document them per analog type: Foucher et al. attach a "Limitations and precautions" paragraph and an "Analogy limitation" field to every category. Gravity, pressure, atmosphere, radiation, weathering and biology are named explicitly as things natural sites cannot reproduce.

### Cited Findings
- General limit: "there are always – inevitably – some differences between the analogue and the object(s) to which it refers." — [Foucher et al. 2021, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pss.2021.105162)
- Weathering and biology: "rocks on Earth have generally undergone some degree of aqueous or fluvial weathering, metamorphism, oxidation and biological alteration. Most importantly, the diversity of biogeochemical cycling on Earth is uniquely complex in the solar system." — [Foucher et al. 2021, section 2.1.1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pss.2021.105162)
- Environment: "biological activity, surface temperature, atmospheric composition, pressure and humidity, magnetic fields and gravity are among the many parameters that can play a role in the resulting protocols and in the physical properties of geological structures themselves." — [Foucher et al. 2021, section 2.1.1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pss.2021.105162)
- Lithology mismatch at impact sites: "the country rock surrounding meteoritic impact structures is rarely an analogue for similar structures on extra-terrestrial bodies." — [Foucher et al. 2021, section 2.1.1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pss.2021.105162)
- Process mismatch: formational processes may differ; "The best example ... is the use of nuclear bomb explosion craters as analogues of impact craters to train astronauts." — [Foucher et al. 2021, section 2.1.1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pss.2021.105162)
- Engineering sites: "Environmental conditions, such as atmospheric composition, pressure, gravity and radiation are difficult or impossible to mimic in laboratory setups and cannot be reproduced in natural sites." — [Foucher et al. 2021, section 2.1.5](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pss.2021.105162)
- Material properties: volcanic analog sites "may not necessarily correspond to extraterrestrial volcanics in terms of their mechanical properties (porosity, density, etc.) or detailed composition (e.g. Martian basalts are enriched in iron compared to analogous terrestrial basalts)." — [Foucher et al. 2021, section 2.1.3](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pss.2021.105162)
- Regolith simulants: rock and regolith textures "are typically defined by geological processes too complex to mimic in laboratory settings" (citing Taylor, Pieters & Britt 2016, "Evaluations of lunar regolith simulants", PSS 126, 1-7). — [Foucher et al. 2021, section 2.2.1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pss.2021.105162)
- Partial-environment facilities exist only off-site: simulation chambers for temperature, pressure, humidity, dust and radiation; drop towers and parabolic flights for gravity. — [Foucher et al. 2021, section 2.1.5](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pss.2021.105162)
- Documentation method used by Foucher et al.: each analog type is described under three fixed headings, Relevance / Uses / Limitations and precautions, summarised in a table with an explicit "Analogy limitation" row (table in section 2 above). — [Foucher et al. 2021, Fig. 1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pss.2021.105162)
- CAFE: "no single analogue can provide an identical representation of landforms, materials and processes found on the Moon and Mars." — [CAFE Executive Summary](https://esamultimedia.esa.int/docs/gsp/C4000104716ExS.pdf)
- Preston & Dartnell: "no analogue site is a perfect representation of another planet or moon." — [Preston & Dartnell 2014](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/244C9EB88B74D6905133DD2228DD3112/core-reader)
- Site-level limits documented by Lambert & Reimold: Clearwater West and Mistastin largely underwater; young small craters (Meteor Crater, Talemzane, Steinheim) lack the full range of impact features; old large structures (Vredefort, Sudbury) are deeply eroded; Ries crater fill is covered by lake deposits; Steinheim's ejecta are lost. — [Lambert & Reimold 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspas.2023.1186173)
- Reduced gravity on Earth is available only briefly (parabolic flights, drop towers, neutral buoyancy, cable offloading) and offloading does not reproduce uniform force at the wheel-regolith interface. **[UNVERIFIED, search snippet of a 2026 arXiv review]** — [arXiv:2608.21358](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.21358)
- Lunar dust is sharper than terrestrial sand because nothing erodes it (popular-science statement). **[UNVERIFIED, search snippet]** — [Popular Science](https://www.popsci.com/live-on-the-moon/)
- DEM-specific limit reported by a competing team: Copernicus DEM heights are surface heights that include vegetation and buildings; illumination conditions are not represented by terrain metrics. — [GitHub, shishir-kuet/terrestrial-analog-finder](https://github.com/shishir-kuet/terrestrial-analog-finder)

### Inferences
- A defensible "where the analogy breaks" block per candidate site can copy Foucher's structure: (1) properties matched and measured (topography, slope, roughness, landform class); (2) properties partly matched (lithology, aridity, temperature range); (3) properties that Earth cannot match anywhere and that are therefore reported but never scored (1/6 g, vacuum, radiation, regolith electrostatics and unweathered angular grains, extreme thermal cycling, absence of water and biology).
- Vegetation, water, snow/ice cover and human structures are the Earth-specific contaminants of a DEM comparison and should be handled as masks or penalties, justified by Foucher's weathering/biology limitation.

### Gaps
- No primary NASA or ESA document was opened that enumerates lunar-analog limits item by item (gravity, vacuum, radiation, electrostatic dust, thermal extremes). Electrostatic dust behaviour and regolith abrasiveness are supported here only by a snippet and by the citation to Taylor et al. 2016, which I did not open.
- No published framework was found that gives a NUMERIC penalty or fidelity discount for unmatched properties; all sources document them qualitatively.
